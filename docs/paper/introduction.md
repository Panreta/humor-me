# Introduction

Large language models can write a passable joke on request, yet stand-up comedy is not a list of jokes. A comedian performs to a particular room, notices what lands and what falls flat, and changes course in the middle of a set. Over many shows, the comedian also learns which material suits which audience, so that the cynical bit that works in a late-night bar is dropped at a company party. This feedback loop is reinforcement learning in all but name: the comedian takes an action, the audience returns a reward in the form of laughter, and later choices change in response. Recent work on computational humor improves what a model writes, either by combining several comedic personas [HumorGen] or by optimizing against preferences from a frozen judge [Wang et al., 2025]. These methods evaluate jokes offline, one at a time. None of them performs for an audience, listens to the response, or carries what it learned into the next performance.

In this paper we present humor-me, a voice-only stand-up agent that reads the room through laughter and adapts its material within and across shows. The agent has a single identity, a self-aware and slightly anxious robot, and performs a set of short bits. Before each bit, a director chooses one of six comedic modes drawn from HumorGen [HumorGen]: neurotic, cynic, observer, wordsmith, optimist and absurdist. The modes are styles that the one identity draws on, not separate characters. The director treats this choice as a contextual bandit problem. The context is a coarse description of the room, namely its venue, energy and age, and the director uses LinUCB [Li et al., 2010] so that what it learns in one room transfers to rooms that resemble it. A language model then writes jokes in the chosen mode, a safety filter removes any line that targets the bodies, appearance or protected attributes of people present, and a text-to-speech model [Kokoro] performs the result. The agent works from audio alone and never sees the audience.

Each joke receives a laugh score from zero to three, which serves as the reward for the director. At present a human operator records this score during the show; an automatic laugh detector is planned to replace the operator. We distinguish the training signal from the outcome we report. The per-joke laugh score drives learning, while the audience's rating of the whole show measures success, because raw laugh counts can be inflated by telling more jokes and are confounded by the size of the room. Every show is logged in enough detail to be replayed, so the logs serve both as the director's memory across shows and as a dataset for later training of the joke writer.

Our central hypothesis is that choosing the comedic mode from live laughter yields more laughter and higher show ratings than performing a single fixed persona or switching modes at random. We test this hypothesis in two settings. The first is a simulated audience with a hidden taste for each mode in each kind of room, which allows us to compare policies over hundreds of shows under identical random seeds. The second is the live system performing in front of real people.

In simulation, the adaptive director collects 97% of the laughter obtained by an oracle that knows each room's true preferences, and 18% more than a policy that always performs in the neurotic mode, which an LLM judge rated the funniest. Most of this gain comes from transferring knowledge between similar rooms rather than from the director's initial guess. The result holds when the simulated tastes are replaced with tastes estimated from human votes on online jokes, where the director reaches 96% of the oracle. Two limitations qualify these findings. First, the simulated tastes are assumptions, so the simulator can rank policies but cannot establish that the agent is funny. Second, the director recognizes rooms but not a room whose taste changes during a show: after such a shift, its share of the oracle falls from 95% to 80%, and neither richer state representations nor planning within the show closed this gap in our experiments. On the live side, the complete system passed an acceptance test that included a forty-joke show in front of a real audience. Its safety filter rejected 29 of 30 previously unseen, indirectly harmful lines, and the reward noise measured in the live show was lower than the level the simulator assumes.

This work makes four contributions. First, we build a closed-loop stand-up agent that performs to a room, listens for laughter and learns across shows by combining persona-based generation with contextual-bandit selection. Second, we introduce a simulated audience and an evaluation protocol that compares policies against an oracle on paired seeds, with every success criterion fixed before the experiment runs. Third, we provide empirical evidence that learning across rooms recovers nearly all of the achievable laughter, while adapting to a room whose taste changes within a show remains an open problem. Fourth, we release a live system whose replayable show logs supply preference data for training the joke writer with direct preference optimization [Rafailov et al., 2023], following the broader program of learning from human preferences [Christiano et al., 2017].
